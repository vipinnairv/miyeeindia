Part 17. Four Trades, Start To Finish

Every part so far taught pieces. This part runs four complete trades from the first thought to the closing entry in the journal, with every rupee accounted for. Two of them lose money, because a guide that only shows winners teaches nothing useful.

The prices are illustrative. The arithmetic and the decisions are exactly what you would do.

17.1 Case One: A Breakout That Worked

The idea

A mid cap engineering company. Sales have grown for four years, ROCE has averaged nineteen percent, debt to equity is 0.3, and cash from operations has averaged eighty six percent of operating profit. It is a business worth owning, which is the first filter.

On the chart it has traded between 604 and 648 for eleven weeks, a range of about seven percent. Volume has fallen steadily through the base. ADX is 16, which says no trend yet, which is exactly right for a base.

The trigger

On a Tuesday it closes at 652, above the eleven week high, on volume three point one times its twenty day average. The candle range is more than twice the recent daily average. All four base breakout conditions from Part 6.9 are present.

What actually made this work

Not the entry. The entry was one Tuesday and took thirty seconds.

It was the eleven weeks of doing nothing in weeks 2 and 3, the decision to move the stop to break even rather than to take a small profit in week 5, and selling half at the planned target rather than deciding at that moment to hold for more.

Every one of those decisions was made before the trade started. That is the whole method.

Why this is a good trade, not a bad one

The analysis was sound, the setup was valid, and the trade lost money. Both things are true at once, and accepting that is the hardest part of learning to trade.

What matters is that the loss was 1.14 percent rather than 15 percent. The stock went on to fall to 340 over the next two months. A trader without a stop loss would have watched a 5,700 rupee loss become a 24,000 rupee one, and would probably have averaged down somewhere in the middle and made it worse.

The stop loss did not fail. The stop loss was the entire value of the plan.

The rule to remember

Judge a trade by whether you followed the plan, not by whether it made money. Over a hundred trades, plan discipline is the variable you control and the outcome is the variable you do not.

17.3 Case Three: A Bull Call Spread, Managed

A monthly Nifty view. The index has held a rising trendline for six weeks, ADX is 27, and the 50 day average is above the 200 day. You expect a moderate move up over the next three weeks, not a violent one.

A plain call would cost more and need a bigger move. A bull call spread suits a moderate view, which is the honest description of what you actually believe.

The decision that mattered

On day 14 the spread was worth 12,150 out of a possible 14,850 at expiry. Holding for five more days to capture the last 2,700 would have meant carrying full downside risk of the position for a fifth of the reward.

Closing a spread at eighty percent of maximum value, rather than holding to expiry for the last twenty percent, is one of the few genuinely reliable habits in option trading. The last part of the profit is the most expensive to earn, because gamma is at its most violent exactly then.

17.4 Case Four: An Iron Condor Through a Bad Week

Three things this case demonstrates

The exit was decided on day one. The plan said: close if Nifty touches the short strike. On day 13 that happened, and there was nothing to think about. Deciding at that moment, with money at stake and volatility rising, is how a 4,180 loss becomes a 9,750 one.

Volatility hurt as much as direction. VIX rose from 13.4 to 19.6, which raised the value of every option in the structure and worked against a net seller regardless of where Nifty went. That is vega, and it is the part beginners never see coming.

The wings did their job. The loss was capped at 9,750 by construction. The same trade as a naked short strangle would have had no cap at all, and would have needed roughly five times the margin to hold.

A fifty percent win rate produced a profit because the winners were larger than the losers, and the losers were small because each one had a stop or a defined maximum written down before entry. Nothing else in these four cases was clever. There was no prediction that came true, no indicator that called a turn, and no moment of insight.

The rule to remember

This is what competent trading actually looks like. It is repetitive, most of it is waiting, and the good decisions are all made before the money is at risk. If that sounds boring, it is, and that is the point.
