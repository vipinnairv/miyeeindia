PART 5: DEEP DIVE Consolidation of Accounts

Consolidation is the process of combining the financial statements of a parent company and all its subsidiaries, associates, and joint ventures into one unified set of financial statements called Consolidated Financial Statements (CFS). This is mandatory under Ind AS 110 for any company that has subsidiaries.

5.1 Why Consolidation? (The Group Concept)

A large company like Reliance Industries does not operate as a single entity. It operates through hundreds of legal entities: Jio Platforms (telecom), Reliance Retail (retail stores), Reliance Petroleum (refining), and many more. Each entity files its own standalone financial statements. But an investor who buys shares in Reliance Industries wants to know: what is the TOTAL revenue, profit, assets, and liabilities of the ENTIRE Reliance group?

This is what consolidation provides. It treats the entire group of companies as if they were a SINGLE economic entity. The parent’s investment in subsidiaries is replaced with the actual assets and liabilities of those subsidiaries. Inter-company transactions (sales between group companies, loans between group companies) are eliminated because a single entity cannot transact with itself.

5.4 Step-by-Step Consolidation Procedure (Detailed)

Step 1: Add Everything Line by Line

Take each line item of the parent’s financial statements and ADD the corresponding line item of each subsidiary. Revenue of parent + Revenue of subsidiary = Combined Revenue. Similarly for every asset, liability, income, and expense line. At this point, the numbers are just a simple addition.

Step 2: Eliminate the Investment in Subsidiary

In the parent’s standalone balance sheet, there is an asset called “Investment in Subsidiary.” In the subsidiary’s balance sheet, there is Share Capital and Reserves. These are effectively two sides of the same coin the parent’s investment IS the subsidiary’s equity. If we just add them, we would be double-counting. So we eliminate them against each other.

Elimination entry: Debit (remove) the subsidiary’s Share Capital (at acquisition date values), Debit (remove) the subsidiary’s Pre-acquisition Reserves, Debit: Goodwill (the excess of consideration over net assets), Credit (remove) the parent’s “Investment in Subsidiary.”

Step 3: Eliminate ALL Inter-Company Transactions

This is where consolidation gets detailed. Every transaction between group companies must be cancelled out because, from the group perspective, these are internal transactions (you cannot sell to yourself).

3a. Inter-Company Sales and Purchases: If Tata Steel (parent) sells ₹500 crore of steel to Tata Motors (subsidiary), the consolidated accounts eliminate ₹500 crore from Revenue and ₹500 crore from Cost of Materials. The net impact on consolidated profit is zero as it should be, because no external sale has occurred.

3b. Inter-Company Loans and Interest: If the parent lent ₹100 crore to a subsidiary at 10% interest, the parent shows ₹10 crore interest income and the subsidiary shows ₹10 crore interest expense. Both are eliminated. The loan itself (asset in parent’s books, liability in subsidiary’s books) is also eliminated.

3c. Inter-Company Dividends: If the subsidiary pays ₹20 crore dividend to the parent, the parent records dividend income and the subsidiary reduces reserves. In consolidation, this is eliminated because it is an internal transfer of cash within the group.

3d. Unrealised Profit on Inventory (CRITICAL and frequently tested): If a parent sells goods to a subsidiary at a markup, and those goods are STILL in the subsidiary’s inventory at year-end (not yet sold to an external customer), the profit on this internal sale is UNREALISED from the group’s perspective. It must be eliminated.

🏢 Detailed Example: Unrealised Profit Elimination

Tata Steel sells steel worth ₹200 crore to Tata Motors at a gross margin of 25%. So the cost to Tata Steel was ₹150 crore, and the selling price to Tata Motors was ₹200 crore. Tata Steel recognises ₹50 crore profit.

At year-end, 40% of this steel (₹80 crore worth) is still in Tata Motors’ inventory (used as raw material for car manufacturing but not yet consumed).

The unrealised profit in closing inventory = ₹80 crore × 25% markup = ₹20 crore.

Consolidation adjustment: Debit Revenue (or Cost of Sales) ₹20 crore, Credit Inventory ₹20 crore.

This reduces consolidated profit by ₹20 crore and reduces consolidated inventory by ₹20 crore. Without this adjustment, the group’s profit would be overstated because the group has not yet “earned” this profit from an external customer.

Step 4: Recognise Non-Controlling Interest (NCI)

What is NCI? When the parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the remaining portion belongs to outside shareholders called Non-Controlling Interest (NCI) or Minority Interest. For example, if Reliance owns 85% of Jio Platforms, then 15% of Jio belongs to other investors (like Facebook/Meta and Google, who invested in Jio).

Balance Sheet: NCI is shown as a SEPARATE component of Equity (not a liability). NCI = NCI’s share of the subsidiary’s net assets (including post-acquisition profits).

P&L: The total profit of the subsidiary is included in the consolidated P&L (because we add 100% of subsidiary’s revenue and expenses). But then a separate line shows “Profit Attributable to Non-Controlling Interests” to indicate how much of the total profit belongs to minority shareholders. The remaining “Profit Attributable to Owners of the Parent” is what matters to the parent’s shareholders.

Step 5: Equity Method for Associates and Joint Ventures

For entities where the parent has Significant Influence (usually 20%-50% holding) but not control, full consolidation is NOT done. Instead, the Equity Method is used. This shows up as ONE line in the Balance Sheet and ONE line in the P&L.

Balance Sheet “Investment in Associate”: Initially recorded at cost. Then adjusted each year for the investor’s share of the associate’s profit (increases the investment) and dividends received from the associate (decreases the investment, because the dividend is a return of investment, not income).

P&L “Share of Profit of Associates and Joint Ventures”: The investor’s proportionate share of the associate’s net profit is shown as a single line item, usually below operating profit.

🏢 Example: Bharti Airtel and Indus Towers

Bharti Airtel holds approximately 48% of Indus Towers (a telecom tower company). Since Airtel does not have outright control (only significant influence), it uses the Equity Method.

If Indus Towers earns a net profit of ₹2,000 crore: Airtel recognises 48% × ₹2,000 crore = ₹960 crore as “Share of Profit of Associates” in its consolidated P&L. The Investment in Associate on Airtel’s Balance Sheet increases by ₹960 crore.

If Indus Towers pays a dividend of ₹500 crore: Airtel receives 48% × ₹500 crore = ₹240 crore. But this is NOT recorded as income again (because Airtel already picked up its share of profit). Instead, the Investment in Associate decreases by ₹240 crore.

This is the beauty of the equity method it avoids double-counting.
