PART 4: DEEP DIVE Business Combinations (Ind AS 103)

IFRS Equivalent: IFRS 3 | Old AS Equivalent: AS 14 (Accounting for Amalgamations)

Business Combinations mergers, acquisitions, and amalgamations are among the most complex transactions in corporate finance. They generate massive fees for investment banks, consulting firms, law firms, and valuation firms. Understanding Ind AS 103 is essential for anyone in M&A advisory, due diligence, corporate finance, or audit.

4.1 What is a Business Combination?

A Business Combination is a transaction or event in which an acquirer obtains control of one or more businesses. The key word here is “control” as defined by Ind AS 110 (power + exposure to variable returns + ability to use power to affect returns). Business combinations include mergers (two companies merge into one), acquisitions (one company buys a controlling stake in another), amalgamations (one or more companies merge into an existing company), and demergers followed by a combination.

What is NOT a business combination? Acquiring a group of assets that do not constitute a “business” (e.g., buying a plot of land). A business includes inputs, processes, and outputs not just individual assets. Joint arrangement formations are also excluded.

4.2 The Acquisition Method The ONLY Method Allowed

Critical Change from Old AS: Under old AS 14, companies could use the Pooling of Interests Method (simply add up the book values of both companies without any fair value adjustments simpler but less informative) or the Purchase Method. Under Ind AS 103, ONLY the Acquisition Method is allowed. The Pooling of Interests Method is completely BANNED.

Why was Pooling banned? Because it allowed companies to avoid recognising goodwill and avoid fair-valuing the acquired assets. This hid the true cost of acquisitions and the risk of overpayment. By mandating the Acquisition Method, Ind AS 103 forces transparency about what was acquired, what it was worth, and how much premium was paid.

4.2.1 Step 1: Identify the Acquirer

In every business combination, one party must be identified as the acquirer. This is usually the entity that: transfers cash or other assets, issues equity instruments, or is the larger entity. In a straightforward acquisition (Company A buys 100% of Company B for cash), the acquirer is clearly Company A. But in mergers-of-equals or share-swap deals, it can be complex.

Reverse Acquisition: Sometimes the legal acquirer (the company that technically “acquires” the other) is actually the smaller entity. In substance, the acquired company’s shareholders end up controlling the combined entity. In this case, the accounting acquirer (the one treated as acquirer under Ind AS 103) is DIFFERENT from the legal acquirer. This is common when a small listed company acquires a large unlisted company by issuing so many shares that the unlisted company’s shareholders end up owning a majority of the listed entity.

4.2.2 Step 2: Determine the Acquisition Date

The acquisition date is the date on which the acquirer obtains control. This is usually the closing date of the transaction when shares are transferred, consideration is paid, and regulatory approvals (CCI in India, for example) are obtained. All recognition and measurement happens as of this date.

4.2.3 Step 3: Recognise and Measure Identifiable Assets and Liabilities at Fair Value

This is the most complex and time-consuming step. On the acquisition date, the acquirer must:

(a) Identify ALL assets acquired and liabilities assumed including intangible assets that were NOT on the acquiree’s balance sheet. This is critical: many valuable intangible assets like customer relationships, brand names, technology, order backlogs, and favorable contracts do not appear on a company’s standalone balance sheet (because they were internally generated and did not meet Ind AS 38’s capitalisation criteria). But in a business combination, they MUST be separately identified and fair-valued.

(b) Measure each identified asset and liability at its acquisition-date FAIR VALUE (as per Ind AS 113’s fair value measurement framework).

4.2.4 Purchase Price Allocation (PPA) The Complete Process

PPA is the process of allocating the purchase consideration to individual identified assets and liabilities at fair value. This is a major area of work for Big 4 advisory practices, boutique valuation firms, and management consultants.

4.2.5 Step 4: Recognise and Measure Goodwill

Goodwill Formula:

Goodwill = Purchase Consideration Paid + Fair Value of Non-Controlling Interest (NCI) + Fair Value of Any Previously Held Interest (in a step acquisition) MINUS Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets Acquired.

What does Goodwill represent? Goodwill is the premium the acquirer paid over and above the fair value of all identifiable assets and liabilities. It represents factors that are not separately identifiable as assets: expected synergies (cost savings from combining operations), assembled workforce (the team of employees), going concern value (the value of the business as a running enterprise vs the sum of its parts), growth potential and market positioning, and any overpayment (if the acquirer paid too much).

Critical Post-Acquisition Treatment: Goodwill is NOT amortised (unlike old AS 14 where it was amortised over 5 years). Instead, Goodwill is tested for impairment at least once every year under Ind AS 36. It is allocated to Cash Generating Units (CGUs), and if the CGU’s recoverable amount falls below its carrying amount (including goodwill), the goodwill is written down. This impairment loss is recognised in P&L and can NEVER be reversed.

4.5 Complete Numerical Example

🏢 Detailed Example: ABC Ltd. Acquires DEF Ltd.

ABC Ltd. acquires 80% of DEF Ltd. for ₹800 crore in cash. DEF Ltd.’s balance sheet on acquisition date shows: Net Assets at book value = ₹500 crore.

PPA identifies the following fair value adjustments: Land revalued upward by ₹100 crore (book: ₹200 crore, fair value: ₹300 crore). Brand name identified at ₹80 crore (not on DEF’s books). Customer relationships at ₹50 crore (not on DEF’s books). Deferred Tax Liability on fair value adjustments: (₹100 + ₹80 + ₹50) × 25% = ₹57.5 crore.

Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets = ₹500 + ₹100 + ₹80 + ₹50 − ₹57.5 = ₹672.5 crore.

ABC acquired 80%, so its share = 80% × ₹672.5 = ₹538 crore. NCI (20% at proportionate share of net assets) = 20% × ₹672.5 = ₹134.5 crore.

Goodwill = ₹800 (consideration) + ₹134.5 (NCI) − ₹672.5 (net assets) = ₹262 crore.

Balance Sheet Impact: Assets increase by DEF’s fair-valued assets + ₹262 crore goodwill. Liabilities increase by DEF’s liabilities + ₹57.5 crore DTL. NCI of ₹134.5 crore appears in equity. Cash decreases by ₹800 crore.
