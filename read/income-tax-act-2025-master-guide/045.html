Keep a notice register with DIN, issue date, due date, section and status. For years up to FY 2025-26, the 1961 Act procedure continues; check the section quoted in each notice.

42.3 Processing of returns (section 270)

Every return is processed by the Centralised Processing Centre. Adjustments allowed at this stage are limited to: arithmetical errors; incorrect claims apparent from information in the return; disallowance of losses where the return was late; disallowances indicated in the audit report but not made in the return; deductions claimed under certain sections where the return was late; income appearing in Form 168, Form 130 or Form 131 but not included in the return; and inconsistencies with earlier years as prescribed. Before making an adjustment, the CPC must give an intimation and consider the response within 30 days.

42.4 Scrutiny assessment

Stage

Details

Selection

Through computer-based risk parameters or specified criteria (search, survey, information from other agencies)

Notice

Scrutiny notice within 3 months from the end of the tax year in which the return was filed

Faceless procedure

All notices, submissions and hearings through the e-filing portal; personal hearing by video conference on request in specified cases

Show cause

A draft assessment order or show cause notice before any variation prejudicial to the taxpayer

Order

Generally within 12 months from the end of the tax year in which the return was filed (longer where TP reference or other special circumstances apply)

42.5 Responding to a notice: a practical checklist

  • Read the notice fully: section, tax year, DIN, due date, specific queries, and whether it is a verification or a scrutiny.
  • Check the portal for any earlier notices or intimations for the same year.
  • Gather evidence against each query (bank statements, contracts, invoices, confirmations with PAN, Form 168 reconciliation).
  • Draft a point-wise reply, attaching indexed documents. Keep language factual.
  • If more time is needed, seek an adjournment on the portal before the due date, giving reasons.
  • Save acknowledgements and keep the notice register updated.
  • On receiving a show cause or draft order, reply specifically to each proposed addition; this is the last opportunity before the order.

Illustration 148: Bank credits notice

A salaried taxpayer receives a notice asking him to explain cash deposits of Rs. 6 lakh and credits of Rs. 15 lakh in his savings account. He prepares a reconciliation: Rs. 9 lakh salary (matches Form 130), Rs. 4 lakh maturity of an FD (bank certificate), Rs. 2 lakh gift from his father (gift deed and father's bank statement), and Rs. 6 lakh cash deposits from withdrawals made earlier in the year (bank statement showing withdrawals). Each item is linked to documents. The notice is closed without addition.

42.6 Best judgment assessment

If the taxpayer fails to file a return, or to comply with notices, or to produce books, the Assessing Officer can make an assessment to the best of his judgment after giving a show cause notice. The assessment must be based on material, not a guess; but it can be substantial. Penalty and loss of the right to carry forward losses may follow.

Can cover the escaped income and other issues that come to notice in the course of proceedings

Judicial view: Reassessment

CIT v. Kelvinator of India Ltd. (Supreme Court, 2010): reopening requires tangible material; a mere change of opinion on the same facts is not a valid ground.

GKN Driveshafts (India) Ltd. v. ITO (Supreme Court, 2002): the taxpayer is entitled to the reasons for reopening and to have objections decided by a speaking order before the reassessment proceeds.

Union of India v. Ashish Agarwal (Supreme Court, 2022): notices issued under the old procedure after the 2021 amendments were treated as show cause notices under the new procedure, and the department was allowed to proceed under the new law.

Since the 2025 Act carries over the post 2021 scheme (information, show cause, order, notice), these principles on tangible information and fair procedure continue to guide reassessments.

Judicial view: Time limits for reassessment

Union of India v. Rajeev Bansal (Supreme Court, 2024): clarified how the time limits for reassessment notices apply to notices issued during the period covered by the pandemic relaxation law (TOLA), holding that the relaxation extends the time for issuing notices and explaining how the balance of limitation is computed after the Ashish Agarwal show cause stage.

42.8 Rectification and revision

  • Rectification: a mistake apparent from the record (arithmetical errors, wrong application of a clear provision, failure to consider a document on record) can be rectified by the authority on its own or on the taxpayer's application, generally within 4 years from the end of the year in which the order was passed. The application must be decided within 6 months.
  • Revision prejudicial to revenue: the Principal Commissioner can revise an order that is erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of revenue, within 2 years from the end of the year of the order, after hearing the taxpayer.
  • Revision in favour of the taxpayer: on the taxpayer's application, within 1 year of the order, where no appeal has been filed.

Judicial view: Rectification and revision

T.S. Balaram, ITO v. Volkart Brothers (Supreme Court, 1971): a mistake apparent from the record must be obvious and patent; it cannot be something established by a long-drawn process of reasoning on points where there may be two opinions.

Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. v. CIT (Supreme Court, 2000): revision is possible only if the order is both erroneous and prejudicial to the revenue. Where the Assessing Officer has taken one of two possible views, the order cannot be revised merely because the Commissioner prefers the other view.

Chapter summary

• Processing under section 270 is limited to specified adjustments with prior intimation.

• Scrutiny is faceless with show cause before adverse orders.

• Reassessment requires information, show cause, order and notice within time limits.

• Rectification and revision correct mistakes and erroneous orders.
