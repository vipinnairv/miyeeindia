IFRS 7 — Financial Instruments: Disclosures

Indian equivalent: Ind AS 107 — Financial Instruments: Disclosures

Objective and scope

IFRS 7 is the disclosure companion to IFRS 9 — it doesn't change how financial instruments are measured; it dictates what a company must tell readers about the financial risks those instruments create and how they are managed.

Core principle

Numbers alone don't reveal risk. IFRS 7 forces companies to explain, in the notes, how exposed they are to credit risk, liquidity risk and market risk — with both words and numbers — so readers can see the dangers hiding behind the financial instruments on the balance sheet.

Defined terms and technical terminology

Term

Technical meaning

Credit risk

The risk that a party who owes the company money fails to pay.

Liquidity risk

The risk the company cannot meet its own obligations when due for lack of ready cash.

Market risk

The risk that market prices, interest rates or exchange rates move against the company (comprises currency, interest-rate and other price risk).

Sensitivity analysis

A disclosure showing how profit or equity would change for a reasonable movement in a risk variable.

Fair value hierarchy

The three-level ranking (from IFRS 13) of how observable the inputs to a fair value are.

Application and worked illustrations

The two disclosure themes

IFRS 7 requires disclosures under two headings. (1) Significance of financial instruments — carrying amounts by category, fair values, and items affecting profit. (2) Nature and extent of risks — for each of credit, liquidity and market risk, both qualitative (how the entity manages the risk) and quantitative (numbers showing exposure) information.

Worked example 1 — credit and liquidity

ExportCo discloses that its largest customer owes 30% of total receivables (a credit-risk concentration), and provides an aging analysis of overdue amounts and its expected-credit-loss allowance. For liquidity risk, it presents a maturity table of its financial liabilities — how much is due within 1 year, 1–5 years, and beyond — so readers can judge whether cash inflows will cover them.

Worked example 2 — market-risk sensitivity

ExportCo has large variable-rate loans and dollar debt. Under IFRS 7 it discloses a sensitivity analysis: 'A 1% rise in interest rates would reduce annual profit by ₹40 lakh' and 'a 5% fall in the rupee against the dollar would increase finance costs by ₹25 lakh.' These let investors gauge how fragile the profits are — information the raw balance sheet hides.

Common mistake: giving only qualitative risk narrative. IFRS 7 requires quantitative data too — concentrations, maturity tables, and sensitivity figures.

IND AS LINK

Ind AS 107 matches IFRS 7 and works with Ind AS 109. The credit, liquidity and market-risk disclosures are especially critical for Indian banks and NBFCs.

Further worked illustrations

Extra numeric example — a maturity analysis

A company's financial liabilities total ₹5 crore. IFRS 7 requires a maturity table showing undiscounted cash outflows by time band: say ₹1.5 crore due within 1 year, ₹2.5 crore in 1–5 years, ₹1 crore beyond 5 years. A reader can immediately test this against expected cash inflows to judge liquidity risk — the risk the company can't meet payments as they fall due.

INDIAN REGULATORY CONTEXT

For Indian NBFCs and banks, the IFRS 7 (Ind AS 107) maturity analysis is critical because these firms borrow short and lend long. Disclosing the buckets exposes any asset-liability mismatch — a key vulnerability regulators (RBI) and investors scrutinise, especially after episodes of NBFC liquidity stress.

TECHNICAL EDGE CASE

A company discloses that a single customer accounts for 45% of its total receivables. Individually every invoice may look fine, but IFRS 7 requires disclosing this concentration — because if that one customer fails, the impact is severe. Risk hidden in a diversified-looking total must be surfaced when it's actually concentrated.
