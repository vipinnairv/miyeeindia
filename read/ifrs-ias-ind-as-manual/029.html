IFRS 9 - Financial Instruments

Indian equivalent: Ind AS 109 - Financial Instruments

Objective and scope

IFRS 9 governs accounting for financial assets and liabilities - cash, loans, receivables, investments, shares, bonds, derivatives - covering how to classify them, at what value to measure them, how to provide for expected credit losses, and the basics of hedge accounting.

Core principle

Financial instruments are contracts about money. IFRS 9 answers three questions: how do you classify each one, at what value do you carry it, and how do you allow for the risk it won't be repaid? Its headline innovation is the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model - provide for bad debts before they actually go bad.

Defined terms and technical terminology

Term

Technical meaning

Financial instrument

A contract giving rise to a financial asset of one party and a financial liability or equity of another (loans, bonds, shares, derivatives).

Amortised cost

Carrying a financial asset using the effective-interest method - for simple debt held to collect contractual cash flows.

FVTPL (fair value through profit or loss)

Carry at market value with changes in profit - for trading and instruments failing the other tests.

FVOCI (fair value through OCI)

Carry at market value with changes parked in Other Comprehensive Income.

Expected Credit Loss (ECL)

A forward-looking estimate of future defaults recognised in advance, before an actual default occurs.

Effective interest rate

The rate that spreads interest evenly over an instrument's life.

Derivative

A contract whose value derives from an underlying (rate, price, index) - e.g., a forward or option.

Application and worked illustrations

Classifying financial assets

IFRS 9 classifies financial assets using two tests: the business model (why you hold the asset) and the cash-flow characteristics (are the cash flows solely payments of principal and interest?). This produces three buckets: amortised cost (held to collect, simple cash flows - most loans and receivables), FVOCI (held to collect and sell), and FVTPL (everything else, including trading assets and equity investments unless an FVOCI election is made). Financial liabilities are mostly at amortised cost, except trading liabilities and derivatives (FVTPL).

The Expected Credit Loss revolution

The old rule (IAS 39) recorded bad-debt losses only after a default was evident - 'too little, too late', as the 2008 crisis showed. IFRS 9 flips this to a forward-looking model with three stages: Stage 1 - from day one, recognise 12-month expected losses; Stage 2 - if credit risk rises significantly, recognise lifetime expected losses; Stage 3 - for credit-impaired assets, lifetime losses with interest on the net amount. So a company records provisions before any specific customer defaults.

Worked example 1 - ECL on receivables

A company has ₹1 crore of trade receivables. Using a simplified approach (allowed for trade receivables), history and forward-looking data suggest lifetime losses of 2%. It records a ₹2 lakh ECL provision immediately, even before any customer misses a payment.

Worked example 2 - an equity investment

A company buys ₹5 lakh of listed shares to trade short-term → FVTPL: at year-end, if worth ₹5.4 lakh, the ₹40,000 gain goes to profit. If instead it holds shares as a long-term strategic stake, it may irrevocably elect FVOCI, so the ₹40,000 sits in OCI and never inflates profit (and, unusually, is not recycled to profit on sale).

Common mistake: waiting for an actual default before providing for a loss. IFRS 9 requires expected losses from initial recognition.

IND AS LINK

Ind AS 109 mirrors IFRS 9, including the ECL model. This is especially significant for Indian banks and NBFCs, which now provide for expected (not merely incurred) loan losses - a major shift from the old incurred-loss regime.

Further worked illustrations

Extra numeric example - a three-stage ECL on a loan

A bank lends ₹10,00,000. Stage 1 (healthy): it provides 12-month expected losses, say ₹5,000. If the borrower's credit risk rises significantly → Stage 2: provide lifetime expected losses, say ₹40,000. If the borrower actually defaults → Stage 3: lifetime losses, and interest is now computed on the net (post-provision) amount. The provision escalates as risk rises, long before any write-off.

INDIAN REGULATORY CONTEXT

Ind AS 109's expected-credit-loss model transformed Indian financial-sector accounting. Where RBI's older norms provided for losses only after accounts turned non-performing, ECL requires forward-looking provisions from day one - often raising provisioning and reshaping reported profits for banks and NBFCs.

TECHNICAL EDGE CASE

A company holding bonds at amortised cost changes its business model and now intends to trade them. IFRS 9 allows reclassification only when the business model actually changes (a rare, senior-management-driven event) - and it's applied prospectively from the reclassification date. You cannot cherry-pick reclassifications to manage profit; the trigger is a genuine change in how the assets are managed.

Extended technical analysis

Classification, ECL staging and hedge accounting

IFRS 9 replaced IAS 39 across three workstreams: classification and measurement driven by business model and cash flow characteristics; the forward-looking expected credit loss impairment model; and a principles-based hedge accounting model aligned to risk management.

ECL is measured as a probability-weighted estimate of credit losses over the expected life, discounted at the original effective interest rate, incorporating reasonable and supportable forward-looking information without undue cost or effort. The standard formulation is ECL = PD × LGD × EAD, where PD is probability of default, LGD is loss given default, and EAD is exposure at default. For trade receivables and contract assets without a significant financing component, the simplified approach is mandatory - lifetime ECL is recognised from inception, commonly via a provision matrix by ageing bucket.

Hedge accounting

IFRS 9 aligns hedge accounting with risk management, replacing IAS 39's rigid 80–125% bright line with a qualitative economic relationship test, a requirement that credit risk does not dominate, and an appropriate hedge ratio. Three hedge types exist: a fair value hedge (hedged item remeasured through P&L, offsetting the derivative); a cash flow hedge (effective portion deferred in OCI in the cash flow hedge reserve, recycled when the hedged item affects P&L); and a net investment hedge in a foreign operation.
