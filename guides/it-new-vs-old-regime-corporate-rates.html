New Tax Regime vs Old Tax Regime for FY 2026-27 and Corporate Tax Rates: Slabs, Standard Deduction, Rebate, Deductions Foregone, Section 115BAA and 115BAB

Authored by VipinPublished 18 Aug 2026

A slab-by-slab comparison of the default new regime and the optional old regime for individuals and HUFs, worked examples, the switching rules for salaried and business taxpayers, and the full menu of corporate tax rates.

Read this first: the law changed on 1 April 2026. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 with effect from 1 April 2026. FY 2026-27 is therefore the first tax year under the new Act; the separate concepts of "previous year" and "assessment year" have been replaced by a single "tax year", so what practitioners would have called AY 2027-28 is now simply the tax year 2026-27, with returns filed in 2027. The new Act largely carries forward the substance of the old provisions but renumbers and regroups them. In this guide, section numbers of the 1961 Act are used as familiar reference labels (for example "Section 44AD") because most forms, case law, software and professionals still use them; the corresponding provision in the 2025 Act should be confirmed using the official section mapping published on incometax.gov.in. Returns for FY 2025-26 filed during 2026 remain governed by the 1961 Act.

1. Statutory Overview and Legislative Intent

For decades, India's personal income tax system combined relatively high slab rates with a long list of exemptions and deductions that rewarded particular kinds of saving and spending. The Finance Act, 2020 introduced an alternative concessional regime in Section 115BAC with lower rates but almost no deductions. The Finance Act, 2023 made it the default regime from FY 2023-24, and the Finance Act, 2025 reworked the slabs and raised the rebate so that resident individuals with total income up to Rs. 12 lakh pay no tax. The policy intent is to simplify, reduce compliance and leave saving decisions to the taxpayer.

The Income-tax Act, 2025 carries the new regime forward as the default (in Section 202 of the new Act), with the old structure available by choice subject to conditions. For companies, the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019 introduced concessional rates in Sections 115BAA (22% for existing domestic companies that forgo specified incentives) and 115BAB (15% for new manufacturing companies, a window that has now closed for new entrants).

Who the regimes apply to

  • New regime (default): individuals, HUFs, associations of persons (other than co-operative societies), bodies of individuals and artificial juridical persons.
  • Old regime (optional): available on exercising the option within the prescribed time and manner.
  • Companies: normal rates, or the concessional regimes on exercising the option in the prescribed form.

3. Step-by-Step Compliance and Filing Workflow

A. Salaried employees

  1. April: declare the intended regime to the employer. If no declaration is made, the employer deducts TDS under Section 192 as per the new (default) regime, as clarified by CBDT Circular No. 04/2023.
  2. During the year: under the old regime, submit investment declarations and, by January or February, proofs in Form 12BB (rent receipts and landlord PAN where rent exceeds Rs. 1 lakh a year, LTC bills, home loan interest certificate, 80C and 80D proofs).
  3. After year end: obtain Form 16 by 15 June, download the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS) from incometax.gov.in, and reconcile salary, interest, dividends and capital gains.
  4. Choose the final regime in the return. A salaried person without business income may choose either regime afresh each year in the return of income, filed by the due date, even if a different regime was declared to the employer.
  5. File the return on the e-filing portal (ITR-1 or ITR-2 as applicable), and e-verify within 30 days using Aadhaar OTP, net banking, bank or demat account EVC, or DSC.

B. Individuals and HUFs with business or professional income

  1. The new regime applies by default. To opt for the old regime, file Form 10-IEA on or before the due date for filing the return of income for that year.
  2. Once opted out, the taxpayer can re-enter the new regime only once in a lifetime (by withdrawing the option in Form 10-IEA). After that, the old regime cannot be chosen again while business income exists.
  3. Compute advance tax liability under the chosen regime and pay instalments on time to avoid interest under Sections 234B and 234C.

C. Companies

  1. Evaluate the 115BAA option: compare normal tax with MAT, available MAT credit, and the deductions that would be lost (especially SEZ and Section 80-IA type incentives).
  2. File Form 10-IC on or before the due date of filing the return for the first year in which the option is exercised. Form 10-ID applied for Section 115BAB.
  3. Report the option in the return (ITR-6) and in the tax audit report. Advance tax and MAT computations change accordingly.

E. Marginal relief just above Rs. 12 lakh

Assume a resident salaried individual under the new regime with gross salary of Rs. 12,85,000 and no other income. Taxable income after the standard deduction of Rs. 75,000 is Rs. 12,10,000, which is just above the rebate limit, so the Section 87A rebate is not available in the normal way. Tax on slabs would be Rs. 61,500 (Rs. 20,000 on the 4 to 8 lakh slab, Rs. 40,000 on the 8 to 12 lakh slab, and Rs. 1,500 at 15% on the Rs. 10,000 above Rs. 12 lakh). Marginal relief limits the tax to the income exceeding Rs. 12 lakh, that is Rs. 10,000. Tax payable is therefore Rs. 10,000 plus cess of Rs. 400, a total of Rs. 10,400, not Rs. 63,960. Payroll software must apply this relief in TDS computations, otherwise employees near the threshold suffer excess deduction.

F. When the old regime still wins

Consider gross salary of Rs. 24 lakh with HRA exemption of Rs. 3,60,000 (high rent in a metro), Section 80C of Rs. 1,50,000, Section 80D of Rs. 75,000 (self and senior citizen parents), Section 80CCD(1B) of Rs. 50,000 and interest on a self-occupied home loan of Rs. 2,00,000. Under the old regime the deductions, including the standard deduction of Rs. 50,000, total Rs. 8,85,000 and taxable income is Rs. 15,15,000. Under the new regime, taxable income is Rs. 23,25,000. Old regime tax: Rs. 1,12,500 on income up to Rs. 10 lakh plus Rs. 1,54,500 at 30% on Rs. 5,15,000, total Rs. 2,67,000 before cess. New regime tax: Rs. 20,000 plus Rs. 40,000 plus Rs. 60,000 plus Rs. 80,000 plus Rs. 81,250 (25% on Rs. 3,25,000), total Rs. 2,81,250 before cess. Here the old regime saves about Rs. 14,250 before cess. The lesson: taxpayers with large HRA, home loan interest and full use of other deductions should compute both.

6. Practical Audit Pitfalls and Best Practices

  • Run both computations every year. Changes in salary, rent, loans and family health insurance can flip the answer.
  • Remember special-rate incomes and rebate. A taxpayer with Rs. 11 lakh of salary income and Rs. 2 lakh of short-term capital gains under Section 111A cannot use the Section 87A rebate against tax on the capital gains.
  • Business taxpayers must calendar Form 10-IEA. Missing the form means the default regime applies, and the switch-back rule limits future flexibility.
  • Check employer NPS structuring. Section 80CCD(2) is one of the few deductions available in the new regime and can be built into salary structures.
  • For companies, model MAT credit. A company with large MAT credit may benefit from deferring the move to Section 115BAA until the credit is used.
  • Keep evidence for old regime claims. The department matches HRA claims with landlord PANs, and deduction claims with AIS data. Unsupported claims are a major source of notices under the e-verification scheme.
  • Watch transition mapping. From tax year 2026-27, cite the provisions of the Income-tax Act, 2025 in declarations and correspondence, using the official mapping, especially in employer forms and board resolutions.
